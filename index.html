Things to Know Before Moving to Scottsdale

Scottsdale, Arizona, consistently ranks as one of the most desirable places to live in the American Southwest. With stunning desert landscapes, a vibrant arts scene, and year round sunshine, it draws families, retirees, remote workers, and luxury buyers. But relocating here requires more than booking a moving truck from summer heat to the housing market, real insights make the transition smooth.

Understanding Scottsdale’s Unique Personality and Culture

Scottsdale is not a monolithic city. It balances a fascinating duality part Old West charm, part modern luxury. The city’s nickname, “The West’s Most Western Town,” nods to its preservation of cowboy culture, complete with weekly rodeos and historic storefronts in Old Town. At the same time, Scottsdale has become a hub for high-end resorts, world-class golf courses, and contemporary art galleries.

The Scottsdale Lifestyle: More Than Just Resorts

Many outsiders picture Scottsdale as a playground for the wealthy, and while there are certainly affluent pockets, the city offers a lifestyle that accommodates various budgets and preferences. The dominant vibe is health-conscious and outdoorsy. Mornings often start with a hike in the McDowell Sonoran Preserve, followed by a stop at a local coffee shop, and afternoons might be spent at a spa or pool. Evenings bring al fresco dining, thanks to the mild temperatures for most of the year.

Seasonal events and festivals fill the calendar from the Scottsdale ArtWalk to the Arabian Horse Show. The city also has a growing craft beer and culinary scene that extends beyond resort restaurants. For those moving to Scottsdale, embracing an active, outdoor-oriented lifestyle is almost a prerequisite, as much of the city’s social fabric revolves around enjoying the natural environment.

What to Expect from Scottsdale Weather Year-Round

The weather is arguably the most talked-about aspect of living in Scottsdale Arizona, and for good reason. With more than 300 sunny days per year, outdoor plans rarely get rained out. However, the flip side of that sunshine is extreme summer heat.

Summer Realities

From June through September, daytime high temperatures regularly exceed 100°F (38°C), often climbing to 110°F or higher. Locals plan outdoor activities for early morning or after sunset. The heat is dry, which makes it slightly more bearable than humid climates, but it still demands respect. Newcomers should expect to rely heavily on air conditioning, stay hydrated, and learn to cover car steering wheels with towels to avoid burns.

The Other Seasons

Winter (November to February) is the payoff for enduring summer. Daytime highs range from the mid-60s to low 70s, with crisp, cool nights. This is peak season for snowbirds (seasonal residents from colder states) and tourists. Spring and fall bring near-perfect conditions warm days, cool evenings, and low humidity. Monsoon season typically arrives in July and August, bringing dramatic thunderstorms, dust storms (haboobs), and sudden downpours that can cause localized flooding.

For anyone relocating to Scottsdale, preparing for the climate means choosing appropriate clothing, planning for higher summer utility bills, and ensuring a vehicle is equipped for occasional dust storms.

The True Cost of Living in Scottsdale

One of the first questions future residents ask is, “How expensive is it really?” The answer: above the national average, but often more affordable than coastal metropolitan areas like Los Angeles, San Francisco, or New York City.

Housing as the Main Driver

Housing represents the largest expense. As of recent data, the median home price in Scottsdale hovers well above the national median, though prices vary dramatically by neighborhood. Rentals are also competitive, particularly during peak season. Those moving to Scottsdale should budget accordingly and be prepared for a fast-moving market.

Utilities, Groceries, and Transportation

Electricity bills spike in summer due to air conditioning, often doubling or tripling winter rates. Water costs are moderate but important in the desert. Groceries and dining out tend to be slightly higher than the national average, especially at upscale restaurants. On the positive side, Arizona has relatively low property taxes compared to many states, and there is no state estate tax.

Transportation costs can be lower for those who work remotely or live near their daily needs, as Scottsdale is car-dependent. Gas prices generally align with national averages. Public transit exists (Valley Metro buses and a light rail extension into Mesa), but most residents rely on personal vehicles.

Comparing to Other Arizona Cities

Scottsdale is notably more expensive than neighboring Phoenix, Mesa, or Glendale. However, many argue the premium pays for better-maintained public spaces, lower crime rates, and higher-end amenities. For anyone comparing cost of living in Scottsdale to other Sun Belt destinations, it sits roughly on par with cities like Denver or Austin, but with lower state income tax.

Homes in Scottsdale range from modest mid-century ranch houses to multimillion-dollar desert modern masterpieces. Top Scottsdale Neighborhoods for Every Lifestyle

Scottsdale is a city of distinct villages and communities, each with its own character. Choosing the right neighborhood can define the entire living experience.

Old Town Scottsdale — The historic heart of the city, walkable, vibrant, and artsy. It offers a mix of renovated bungalows, new condos, and loft apartments. Best for young professionals, empty nesters, and those who want an urban-desert vibe.
McCormick Ranch — One of the first master-planned communities in Arizona, featuring two lakes, miles of greenbelt paths, and a mix of single-family homes and townhomes. Popular with families and outdoor enthusiasts.
North Scottsdale (DC Ranch, Silverleaf, Desert Mountain) Synonymous with luxury living: gated communities, golf courses by famous architects, and panoramic mountain views. Best for affluent buyers, retirees, and those seeking privacy.
Gainey Ranch — Resort-style living with canal paths, upscale shopping at The Shops at Gainey Village, and easy freeway access. A favorite among professionals and families.
South Scottsdale — More affordable, featuring smaller mid-century homes. Undergoing gradual revitalization with new breweries and coffee shops. Good entry point for first-time buyers or renters.

When evaluating best areas in Scottsdale, visitors should drive through neighborhoods at different times of day, check commute times, and consider proximity to schools, healthcare, and entertainment.

Scottsdale Schools and Education Options

Families moving with children often prioritize school quality. Scottsdale is served primarily by the Scottsdale Unified School District (SUSD), which has a strong reputation overall, though performance varies by individual school.

Public Schools

SUSD operates over 20 elementary schools, several middle schools, and four comprehensive high schools (Arcadia, Chaparral, Coronado, and Desert Mountain). Chaparral and Desert Mountain high schools are particularly well-regarded, with high test scores and robust extracurriculars. There are also specialized programs, such as the International Baccalaureate (IB) at Saguaro High School and the Scottsdale School for the Arts.

Charter and Private Schools

Arizona is known for its school choice options. Charter schools like BASIS Scottsdale (frequently ranked among the top high schools nationally) and Great Hearts Academies offer rigorous curricula. Private institutions include Phoenix Country Day School, Notre Dame Preparatory, and Rancho Solano Preparatory School. Many of these have waiting lists, so early research is essential. For families, the connection between neighborhood and school quality is strong. Homes in attendance zones for top-rated schools often command premium prices.

Healthcare Facilities and Access

Access to quality healthcare is a major consideration, especially for retirees moving to Scottsdale. The city is well-served by major hospital systems. HonorHealth is the dominant local provider, operating several hospitals including HonorHealth Scottsdale Osborn Medical Center and HonorHealth Scottsdale Thompson Peak Medical Center. Mayo Clinic also has a significant campus in North Scottsdale, including a full-service hospital and specialty clinics — a major draw for those with complex medical needs. Additionally, Banner Health has facilities nearby in Phoenix and Mesa. Specialty care, urgent care centers, and dental offices are abundant throughout the city.

Getting Around Scottsdale: Transportation and Commuting

Scottsdale was designed around the automobile. Sidewalks and bike lanes exist in many areas, but the city’s spread-out nature makes walking impractical for most daily errands. Major arteries include Scottsdale Road, Hayden Road, Pima Road, and the Loop 101 (Pima Freeway). Rush hour traffic can be congested but compared to larger metros, delays are manageable. Valley Metro operates bus routes, though frequency is limited on weekends. The light rail stops just west of Scottsdale, so a car is still necessary. Ridesharing and Waymo autonomous service are available. Phoenix Sky Harbor International Airport is only about 15–25 minutes from most Scottsdale locations, making air travel exceptionally convenient.

Outdoor Recreation and Year-Round Activities

Living in Scottsdale means living outdoors at least during the cooler months. The McDowell Sonoran Preserve covers over 30,000 acres with more than 200 miles of trails. Popular hikes include Tom’s Thumb, Gateway Loop, and Lost Dog Wash. The Indian Bend Wash greenbelt is an 11-mile linear park with paved paths, lakes, and golf courses. Scottsdale is a global golf destination with over 50 public and private courses. Spring training baseball (San Francisco Giants at Scottsdale Stadium) is a cherished local tradition.

Dining, Shopping, and Entertainment Scene

Scottsdale punches above its weight in culinary and retail offerings. From casual Mexican eateries to James Beard Award-winning chefs, the dining scene satisfies all cravings. Fashion Square is one of the largest shopping malls in the Southwest with luxury brands, while Old Town’s art galleries and boutiques sell Native American jewelry and local crafts. The Scottsdale Center for the Performing Arts, SMoCA, and a thriving craft beer scene round out the cultural experience.

Safety and Crime Rates in Scottsdale

Scottsdale consistently ranks as one of the safest large cities in the Phoenix metro area. Violent crime rates are significantly below national averages, though property crime occurs at rates similar to other affluent suburban cities. Most neighborhoods are very safe, with residents comfortable walking after dark in areas like McCormick Ranch and North Scottsdale. Taking basic precautions is sufficient.

Pros and Cons of Relocating to Scottsdale

✓ Pros

  • Abundant sunshine and mild winters
  • High-quality healthcare and education options
  • Diverse outdoor recreation, from hiking to golf
  • Strong job market (healthcare, real estate, technology)
  • Low state income tax (2.5% flat rate)
  • Clean, well-maintained public spaces

✗ Cons

  • Extreme summer heat, limiting outdoor activity for months
  • Higher cost of living than surrounding Arizona cities
  • Car-dependent lifestyle with limited public transit
  • Seasonal tourism crowds (winter and spring)
  • Risk of dust storms and occasional flash floods

For many, the pros far outweigh the cons, especially for those who can work remotely or retire and fully embrace the desert lifestyle.

Frequently Asked Questions About Moving to Scottsdale

What is the best time of year to move to Scottsdale?
The ideal time is between October and April, when temperatures are mild. Moving during summer (June–August) is possible but challenging due to extreme heat.
How much money do I need to live comfortably in Scottsdale?
A single person without children might need $55,000–$70,000 per year. A family of four should budget $100,000–$140,000 annually for a comfortable middle-class lifestyle.
Is Scottsdale a good place to raise a family?
Yes, many families choose Scottsdale for its highly rated schools, low crime rates, parks, and family-oriented events. The biggest consideration is housing affordability.
Do I need a car if I live in Scottsdale?
Almost certainly. While ridesharing is available, the city’s layout and limited public transit make a personal vehicle essential for errands and commuting.
How bad is the summer heat really?
Newcomers often underestimate it. From late June through August, outdoor activity is best limited to early mornings or after sunset. However, air conditioning and pools make indoor life comfortable.
Are there any dangerous areas in Scottsdale?
Scottsdale is generally very safe. Areas with slightly higher property crime tend to be near South Scottsdale and Old Town, but violent crime is rare citywide.
What is the job market like in Scottsdale?
Major employers include healthcare systems (HonorHealth, Mayo Clinic), financial services (Vanguard, Fidelity), technology (GoDaddy, Axon), and tourism/hospitality. Remote work is also common.
